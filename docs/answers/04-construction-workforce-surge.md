# 4. The construction-workforce surge

Asked by brownfield-compute (2026-11-16). The bigger newcomer shock comes before the flats: the
gas plant is being built now, and AWS's 39-building campus is to be built over 2026-2036. **What
does a construction surge do to a small town, and what does that mean for when each phase can
happen?** Case to check: the North Dakota Bakken boom.

Evidence here is real cases and research, not the simulation. Brownfield's own numbers (pass 4):
about 120 renter households near the campus, and 16 homes for rent in Homer City.

## Short answer

A surge hits renters first and hardest, and the town's costs arrive before its tax money does.
Then it ends, and housing built for the peak sits empty. Towns do recover. So:
- hire locally to shrink the surge;
- house the peak in removable lodging the operator pays for, away from the town's rentals;
- size permanent housing to the long-run jobs, not the peak;
- open the first protected flats before the peak;
- put money for police, ambulances, schools and roads in place before the workers arrive.

## What happens, and the cases behind it

1. **Renters get squeezed first.**
   - **Abilene, Texas (Stargate AI data center, 2024-2027).** Oracle cited 9,000 construction
     jobs. Average rent reached almost $2,600, up nearly 50% in a year (Zillow). More than 100
     university students had nowhere to live. Landlords didn't renew leases so homes could become
     short-term rentals. A shelter's time to rehouse a family went from about a month to as long
     as six months (Texas Public Radio/KUT, Aug 2026). RV parks multiplied, and some were
     overbuilt and now aren't filling.
   - **Williston, North Dakota (Bakken, early 2010s).** The population roughly doubled; two-bedroom
     rents passed $3,000 a month; most workers lived in "man camps". Homelessness in North Dakota
     nearly tripled, and police struggled to hire because officers couldn't find housing (news
     reports; a North Dakota State University study of policing found calls for help soared).
2. **Costs come before the tax money.** In Watford City and Dunn County, the early boom brought
   "rapidly expanding service demands and insufficient revenue" (Raimi & Newell 2016, Resources for
   the Future). North Dakota created an energy impact grant fund, but Watford City got $10 million
   of the $190 million it asked for in 2013. It borrowed through bonds and state-bank loans, and
   in 2026 it's still seeking debt relief.
3. **Then the bust, and housing built for the peak sits empty.** When oil prices fell in 2015,
   oil-patch rents dropped 15-20% in two months and new apartment buildings struggled to fill
   (Reuters via ProBuilder). Abilene's RV parks are already showing it.
4. **Towns recover.** In Delta, Utah, a power-plant boom town followed for 24 years, three of four
   measures of how residents felt about their community (satisfaction, belonging) were back to or
   above pre-boom levels about ten years after the boom (Brown, Dorius & Krannich 2005, Rural
   Sociology). The harm is real but temporary, so the job is to get people through it.
5. **Hiring locally shrinks the surge.** Homer City's gas plant had over 1,800 workers on site by
   September 2026 (about 2,000 expected by year end). The company says about 95% of the skilled
   direct-hire craft workforce is from the local area. That's a company claim, and "local" isn't
   defined. People who already live nearby need no new housing.
6. **Worker housing can become a town.** The Tennessee Valley Authority built Norris, Tennessee
   in 1933-34 to house the dam's construction workers, as a planned town. It was sold in 1948 and
   ended up with its residents (accounts differ on exactly how). Today, developers are bringing
   modular oilfield-style workforce housing to data-center sites. In Abilene, one provides
   housing plus transport to the site.
7. **A big build competes with homebuilding for the same workers.** In Texas, data centers hiring
   electricians have delayed housing projects (Tom's Hardware/TechSpot, secondary reports). Building
   the flats during the surge will cost more and take longer.
8. **Pennsylvania already ties energy money to local housing.** Act 13 (2012) put a fee on shale
   gas wells. Part goes to local governments, who may spend it on affordable housing, and part
   funds PHFA's PHARE housing fund (at least 30% of PHARE money must go to households below 50% of
   area median income). It's a precedent for an impact fund, not something that applies to a data
   center today.

## What it would change in the design and the timing

- **A local-hire target in the benefits agreement**, with training. Publish the share
  every quarter and say how "local" is defined. Every local hire is one less home needed.
- **Peak lodging the operator and its contractors pay for**, on campus land, modular, removed or
  converted when the build ends, with transport to the site and conduct rules. Not RV parks
  spreading through town, and not workers bidding against renters.
- **Size permanent housing to the long-run jobs** (about 1,000 permanent jobs at the gas plant,
  plus AWS's), not the construction peak.
- **But AWS's build runs ten years.** Workers there for years are neighbours, not visitors. So
  some permanent-quality flats can house long-stay construction workers, then turn over to the
  mixed plan as the build tapers. That's the Norris pattern, inside answer 1's mix rule.
- **Open the first protected flats before the peak**, with first option for current local renters
  (answer 1), so the 120-or-so renter households have somewhere to go if rents jump.
- **An up-front impact fund** for police, ambulance, schools and roads, paid before the surge
  (an advance against the campus property tax, or a fund in the benefits agreement), because tax
  revenue arrives late. Act 13 shows how Pennsylvania has done this before.
- **Short-term rental rules and lease protections** for the build years, as far as Pennsylvania
  law allows (to check). In Abilene, homes turned into short-term rentals.
- **A public dashboard from day one:** rents, vacancies, shelter waits, police and ambulance
  calls, school enrolment, local-hire share. If a measure crosses a set threshold, more of the
  fund is released.
- **Timing:** brownfield's flats compete with the campus for the same trades, so either build
  them before the AWS peak, or budget for a premium and delays.

## How sure we are

**Medium on the pattern.** Boomtowns are well studied, and Bakken, Abilene and Delta point the same
way: renters squeezed, costs before revenue, overbuilding, then recovery. Much of the Bakken and
Abilene detail is news reporting, not studies.

**Low on Homer City's size.** We don't know the peak number of non-local workers. The 95%-local
figure is the company's, and AWS's workforce plan isn't public. Brownfield's model should treat
the non-local peak as an uncertain range.

## Where Melting Pot could help later

This is experiment 1 almost exactly: strangers arriving in a place that depends on a shared
resource. Clean Up with a wave of short-stay newcomers among residents could show whether being
able to see who's been cleaning, and a welcome, keep the river clean while the newcomers are
there. It would point at cases, not replace them.
