# 5. Does the agreement hold?

Asked by brownfield-compute (2026-11-16). Answers 1-4 lean on a community benefits agreement
(CBA): it funds the shared places, the heat network, the impact fund and the housing mix.
**Once the operator is the most powerful party in town, and most of the tax base, what keeps that
agreement honoured?**

Evidence here is real cases and research, not the simulation.

## Short answer

An agreement holds when:
- the government can enforce it, not just a coalition;
- the money is tied to the land and paid early;
- the operator's benefits depend on it delivering, with clawbacks;
- the community side is independent and paid for its time;
- someone independent checks and publishes the numbers;
- it binds whoever owns the campus next.

Without these, agreements with powerful developers have drifted into "maybes and
might-have-beens".

## What happens, and the cases behind it

1. **Private, unenforced agreements fade.** The Atlantic Yards agreement in Brooklyn (2005) was a
   private contract between the developer and eight groups. Critics say most were funded by the
   developer, its targets were aspirational, and no government was a party. One key signatory
   group closed two months after the arena opened. Later reporting called the benefits "a
   patchwork of maybes and might-have-beens" (City Limits 2022; Next City). Much of this comes
   from open critics.
2. **Courts haven't settled whether these agreements can be enforced.** A 2010 law review found
   no federal or state cases squarely addressing it, including who even has standing to sue (Been
   2010, University of Chicago Law Review). Folding the agreement into the government's own
   development agreement means the government can enforce it, not just community groups.
3. **Benefits tied to the land survive the developer leaving.** In Pittsburgh's Hill District, a
   2015 settlement turned 10 years of property taxes on the 28-acre arena site into a fund for the
   neighbourhood, attached to the parcels rather than to a project. When a key developer pulled
   out, the next partners prepaid the 10 years as a $7.2 million lump sum (2021). The Penguins gave
   up their development rights in October 2025, and the fund carries on, run by a 12-member
   community advisory council (Next City, May 2026). An earlier agreement, signed in 2014 but never
   attached to the land plan, led to a lawsuit.
4. **Pay for performance, with clawbacks.** Wisconsin's 2017 Foxconn deal promised 13,000 jobs
   for up to $2.85 billion in tax credits. When the project shrank, the 2021 renegotiation cut it
   to 1,454 jobs and at most $80 million. Credits are paid only after jobs are shown, and the
   state can recover 100% of a year's incentives on default. The promise collapsed, but the
   public's money was protected.
5. **A process law helps, but only partly.** Detroit's Community Benefits Ordinance (2016) forced
   big projects to negotiate with resident councils. It produced agreements on 10 projects with
   160+ benefits, but at least a fifth duplicated what law already required. Residents said
   benefits weren't proportional to project size, and council members needed training and pay
   (Berglund 2021, Journal of the American Planning Association; WRI 2024).
6. **The biggest taxpayer can shrink its own bill.** Data-center owners appealed Cook County
   assessments seeking cuts of 24% to 90% (the assessor defended them in 2026). Amazon appealed its
   Canton, Mississippi data centers' values (denied, 2026). A corrected Apple data-center valuation
   cost Waukee, Iowa's schools about $2.3 million in a year. If the shared places and the impact
   fund ride on the campus property tax, an appeal can quietly cut them.
7. **The powerful party can also control the information.** The Dalles, Oregon, fought a reporter
   for 13 months to keep Google's water use secret as a "trade secret". It settled in December 2022
   and released 10 years of data, and must publish yearly figures. Water use had roughly tripled
   since 2017.
8. **Keep the company out of the government.** Starbase, Texas has a SpaceX vice-president as
   mayor, and people confuse the city with the company (answer 1). In 1898, Pullman's town was
   ordered sold because a company charter didn't cover running a town (answer 1). In both, the
   backstop was a level of government above the company town.

## What it would change in the design

- **Make the agreement part of the township's land-use approval** (the development agreement or
  conditions of approval), with the community coalition also a party. Both can enforce it.
- **Tie the money to the land and pay it early.** Put the payments on the parcels so they bind
  any future owner; Pittsburgh's tax-district structure (a LERTA) is one Pennsylvania tool.
  Prepay the first years into an escrow or the trust (answers 2 and 4).
- **Make the operator's own benefits conditional.** Tax treatment, zoning approvals for later
  phases, and permits to expand depend on delivering, with clawbacks and set damages for missed
  obligations, like Foxconn's 100% recovery.
- **A floor on local payments.** The operator agrees to a minimum yearly payment, or not to
  appeal below an agreed assessment, for the agreement's life. The shared places and impact fund
  shouldn't hang on an appeal. (Logic; legality to check in Pennsylvania.)
- **An independent community side.** The coalition's money comes from the trust or a
  foundation, never directly from the operator. Members get training and are paid for their time
  (Detroit). Seats represent renters, owners and townspeople (answer 1).
- **An independent monitor and public data.** A yearly compliance report by someone the
  community picks. Publish the operator's water, power, noise, heat delivered and local hiring,
  so no fight like The Dalles is needed.
- **It binds whoever owns it next.** Covenants run with the land, and any sale of the campus
  carries the agreement with it.
- **No operator staff in township government or on the trust's majority** (Starbase).
- **Review every five years**, with a set way to change terms that needs the community side's
  consent.

## How sure we are

**Medium.** The cases point the same way: Atlantic Yards' failure, Hill District's durable fund,
Foxconn's clawbacks, Detroit's mixed results. But there are few systematic studies, enforcement is
largely untested in court, and much of the Atlantic Yards record comes from critics. The
assessment-floor idea is logic only. Whether a Pennsylvania township can lock in a floor or tie
the agreement to land approvals this way needs a Pennsylvania land-use lawyer.

## Where Melting Pot could help later

This is the power question in its purest form: does a much stronger player keep its promises?
A game where one player grows strong partway through (Territory, or a custom one) could compare
promises with no enforcement, with monitoring only, and with monitoring plus penalties. It
connects to the project's candidate core question: what keeps the good mix from sliding into a
few owners. It would point at cases, not replace them.
