# 1. Enclave or mixing?

Asked by brownfield-compute (2026-11-16). Its plan puts 100-250 mixed flats and shared amenities
(food hall, courts, rink, fields, makerspace) at the edge of the Homer City data-center campus,
meant for all of Indiana County. **What layout, access and ownership choices keep a nicer new
neighbourhood beside a poorer town from becoming a resented enclave?**

Evidence here is real cases and research, not the simulation.

## Short answer

Being next to each other doesn't make people mix. What helps:

- shared places at the seam between old town and new;
- one set of rules and one residents' voice for everyone;
- a way for townspeople to own a piece;
- the same housing mix in every phase;
- small, regular activities that bring both groups together.

What hurts: a company that runs the town, gates and private roads, separate doors or rules, and
an owners' association that speaks for only some residents.

## What happens, and the cases behind it

1. **Living side by side doesn't make people mix.** In Chicago's mixed-income developments,
   researchers found little interaction across income and race lines (Chaskin & Joseph 2011;
   their 2015 book). Across all of Facebook, about half the gap in friendships between rich and
   poor comes from never sharing the same groups (exposure). The other half comes from not
   befriending each other even when they do (friending bias). Friending bias is higher in larger
   and more diverse groups, and lower in religious groups than in schools and workplaces (Chetty
   et al. 2022, Nature).
2. **Contact still helps when it's real.** Across 515 studies, contact between groups usually
   reduced prejudice. It did more with equal status, shared goals, cooperation, and support from
   whoever runs the place (Pettigrew & Tropp 2006). Most of those studies were not experiments.
3. **"Those who pay make the rules."** In the Chicago developments, the condo owners had an
   association and the renters had none. The owners won cameras, security and new rules, and the
   renters felt watched and shut out. One resident's suggestion was a single residents'
   association with owners, condo residents and renters on the same board (Chaskin, Khare &
   Joseph 2012).
4. **Pullman vs Hershey.** Both company towns were built with good amenities. In Pullman you could
   only rent, there were no town meetings, and in 1894 wages were cut but rents were not. That led
   to the strike. In 1898 the Illinois Supreme Court ordered the company to sell the town; most of
   it changed hands by 1907, with residents getting first option to buy. Hershey let people buy
   their homes and opened its parks to the public. Hershey still had a strike in 1937, so being
   kind is not enough on its own.
5. **Columbia, Maryland drifted.** Columbia started with unusual racial and income mixing.
   Its older villages mixed apartments, townhouses and houses; newer villages were mostly
   single-family homes with little affordable housing, and segregation grew there (NCRC report,
   advocacy group, figures to check).
6. **Reston, Virginia** was open to all incomes and races from 1964, before fair-housing law
   required it. It made walking paths and open space a priority.
7. **Starbase, Texas is the warning case.** SpaceX and its staff bought out most of the village.
   The 2025 city has a SpaceX vice-president as mayor. It gained control of closing roads and
   Boca Chica Beach, and neighbours pushed back. Its own administrator says people confuse the
   city with the company (Planning magazine, Feb 2026).
8. **Tulsa Remote: welcoming newcomers on purpose works.** The programme pays remote workers to
   move and connects them to volunteering, local groups and businesses. Participants report more
   community engagement and are more likely to stay (Choudhury et al., Organization Science;
   Brookings). Planners warn that a haphazard version risks gentrification.
9. **Separate doors cause resentment.** New York banned "poor doors" (separate entrances for
   affordable units) in 2015 after public outrage. The law says affordable units share the same
   entrances and common areas.
10. **Rents in the old town.** In big US cities, large new apartment buildings in low-income
    areas lowered nearby rents by 5-7% (Asquith, Mast & Reed 2023). That's city evidence. A small
    town with a 1,500-2,000-job campus is a different shock (question 4, the Bakken).

## What it would change in the design

- **Put the shared places on the seam**, between the new neighbourhood and the existing town,
  reachable from town streets. Not inside the new neighbourhood.
- **No gates or private roads; one front door per building**, for every kind of unit.
- **One residents' and users' body** for the shared places, with renters, owners and townspeople
  on it. No owner-only association running them.
- **Keep the company out of running the town.** The operator funds things; a trust, co-op or the
  township runs them (this links to question 2).
- **Give the town a stake.** Some flats for sale or co-op, and first option for people who
  already live in Homer City and Indiana County.
- **Make the mix a rule for every phase** (in the community benefits agreement), so later
  phases don't drift the way Columbia's did.
- **Plan small, regular mixed groups:** leagues, teams, makerspace projects, classes, plus a
  Tulsa Remote-style welcome for newcomers. Space alone doesn't create mixing; regular small
  groups do better.
- **Price the amenities for the town**, not the campus. (Logic, not tested here.)

## How sure we are

**Medium.** The direction is consistent across very different evidence. But most of it is case
studies or correlation, and it comes from cities and suburbs, not a small Pennsylvania town next
to a data center. The two strongest pieces are the contact meta-analysis and the Facebook
friendship data. The weakest are the Columbia figures (an advocacy report) and the
amenity-pricing point (no source).

## Where Melting Pot could help later

A game can show which conditions get two groups to look after a shared place together: for
example, Clean Up with "old-timers" and "newcomers" who can or can't see each other's work. It
would tell us which real cases to look at harder. It isn't evidence for the design by itself.
