# 2. Running the shared places

Asked by brownfield-compute (2026-11-16). **Who runs and pays for the makerspace, the courts and
fields (and rink), the winter garden and the arboretum: a co-op, the township, the operator, or a
trust under the community benefits agreement? What keeps them clean and fair? And the ~3 m strips
between narrow townhouses, which can become nobody's alley.**

Evidence here is real cases and research, not the simulation. Follows on from
[01-enclave-or-mixing.md](01-enclave-or-mixing.md): one body for everyone, the operator funds but
doesn't run things.

## Short answer

Money the operator can't switch off, run by a trust whose board includes the people who use the
places. Each place gets its own small users' committee and a paid person in charge. The users
make and enforce the everyday rules, with penalties that start small. And every piece of land
gets an owner, including the strips between the townhouses.

## What happens, and the cases behind it

1. **Shared places last when their users run them by clear rules.** Elinor Ostrom's eight design
   principles for long-lived commons:
   - clear boundaries;
   - rules that fit the place;
   - users help make the rules;
   - monitors who answer to the users;
   - penalties that start small;
   - cheap, quick ways to settle disputes;
   - outside authorities respect the users' right to organize;
   - small groups nested inside bigger ones.

   A review of 91 studies found the principles well supported, but as tendencies, not
   guarantees (Cox, Arnold & Villamayor Tomás 2010). Those studies are mostly forests, fisheries
   and irrigation, not buildings.
2. **Members' fees alone don't keep a makerspace open.** TechShop, a for-profit membership chain,
   closed all its US locations and liquidated in November 2017. Its CEO said they spent years
   propping up "the wrong business model"; an analyst compared it to a gym that always needs more
   members.
3. **Volunteers alone struggle too.** Noisebridge in San Francisco runs on "do-ocracy" (whoever
   does the work decides) plus consensus. A sociologist who studied it describes constant
   "dramas". Members say it once got bad enough that they shut the space down to reboot it.
4. **Volunteer-run with steady public support works.** Australia's Men's Sheds are run by local
   volunteers, with national government funding for the movement for over a decade (Australian
   Government governance review of the Men's Shed Association).
5. **Public makerspaces are free but short-staffed.** Public libraries run many makerspaces, free
   to use with materials at cost. A California State Library report says staffing has made it hard
   to keep them running as intended. In a 2013 survey, staff taught about half the sessions and
   volunteers about a quarter.
6. **Private money flows to the showcase.** The Central Park Conservancy made Central Park
   excellent. Critics say it helped create "a two-tier parks system": private money went to a few
   Manhattan parks while budget cuts hit the Bronx and Queens.
7. **Steady public money with its own vote lasts.** Minneapolis has had an independent, elected
   park board since 1883, with its own tax levy. Seattle voters created a park district in 2014,
   a taxing district for park upkeep and programmes.
8. **Ground with no clear owner gets neglected and misused, and clear control fixes it.** Liverpool
   gated 3,178 back alleys so only the neighbouring homes could use them; burglary fell about 37%
   against a comparison area, with no sign of it simply moving next door (Bowers, Johnson &
   Hirschfield 2004, rated "Effective" by the US CrimeSolutions programme). Zero-lot-line housing
   avoids the leftover strip altogether: each side yard belongs to a home, and the neighbour gets
   a recorded easement to maintain their wall (common in US zoning codes).

## What it would change in the design

**Who pays**
- **Two money streams the operator can't switch off:**
  - an endowment and maintenance reserve, paid up front through the community benefits
    agreement;
  - a dedicated share of the campus property tax, through the township or county, the way
    Minneapolis and Seattle fund parks.
- **The operator's yearly gifts are extras only**, never what keeps the lights on.
- **Small user fees:** materials at cost, hourly court and rink bookings, with low or no fees for
  low-income county residents. Not a membership model that has to keep growing (TechShop).

**Who runs it**
- **A nonprofit trust** (or a Pennsylvania municipal or recreation authority, to check) owns and
  runs the places. Its board includes renters, owners and townspeople, plus a township seat. The
  operator gets one seat, not control.
- **Each place has its own users' committee** under the trust (Ostrom's nested groups): the
  makerspace's members, the leagues for courts and rink, the garden's volunteers.
- **A paid person in charge of each place**, helped by volunteers. Not volunteers alone
  (Noisebridge), and not staff alone (libraries stretched thin).

**Keeping it clean and fair**
- **Makerspace:**
  - a short sign-off before using each tool (the boundary);
  - volunteer hosts for each area who answer to the members' committee (the monitors);
  - clean-up rules the members wrote, with penalties that start small (a reminder, then a
    cleaning shift, then losing a tool sign-off for a while);
  - free open hours for county residents, membership for more.
- **Courts, rink and fields:** a public booking calendar anyone can see. Set aside drop-in hours,
  cap how much any one league can book, and give county youth first pick in some windows.
  Disputes go to the users' committee, quickly.
- **Garden and arboretum:** a paid gardener plus a volunteer "friends" group, paid for from the
  reserve.
- **The trust's money serves the whole county**, so the new neighbourhood's places don't become
  the showcase while the old town's parks run down (the Central Park lesson).

**The townhouse strips**
- **Give every metre an owner.** Split each strip as side yards, or give it to one home with a
  recorded easement for the neighbour to maintain their wall. No common land between homes.
- If a strip has to stay shared, gate it for the adjoining homes only, like Liverpool's alleys.
  That's second best, and it doesn't clash with "no gates" from answer 1: these are back strips
  between homes, not streets.

## How sure we are

**Medium.**
- **Strongest:** the Ostrom principles (a 91-study review) and the alley-gating result (a
  quasi-experiment with a comparison area, rated "Effective"). But the Ostrom studies are about
  natural resources, not makerspaces and courts.
- **Makerspace models:** a handful of cases (TechShop, Noisebridge, Men's Sheds, libraries), not
  studies.
- **Funding:** the pattern of private money flowing to the showcase park is documented in New
  York, but mostly by critics, and the figures are old.
- **No source:** the fee levels and booking caps are logic, not tested.

## Where Melting Pot could help later

The makerspace is a Clean Up problem: the whole space stays usable only if people clean up after
themselves. A game could compare no rules, rules set from above, and rules the players set
themselves with penalties that start small. That would show which real cases to dig into, not
prove the design.
